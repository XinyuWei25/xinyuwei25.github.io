About

I am a PhD candidate in economics at ECARES, Université libre de Bruxelles, supervised by Bram De Rock. I expect to complete my PhD in 2027 and am on the 2026–27 job market.

My research focuses on household risk, labour economics, ageing and social protection. Using household panel data, I study how families respond to income, employment and care shocks, and how these responses affect their welfare.

Before joining ECARES, I studied at the Chinese University of Hong Kong and Georgetown University and worked at the Shenzhen Finance Institute, where I wrote reports on US macroeconomic developments and taught applied econometrics.

When Care Supply Becomes Care Demand: How Older Couples Adjust to Caregiver Decline

Working paper, October 2026Latest version (PDF)
SHARE

A healthy spouse insures older people against long-term care needs, but the caregiving spouse is ageing too. When caregivers develop multiple new limitations, the care relationship becomes about 20 percentage points more likely to end, and recipients become about twice as likely to report unmet care needs.

Abstract

A healthy spouse insures older people against long-term care needs, but the caregiving spouse is aging too. Using SHARE, I follow couples in which one partner cares for the other as the caregiver develops new limitations in activities of daily living. Multiple new limitations make the care relationship about 20 percentage points more likely to end. Help from children and home services rises, but much of it goes to the caregiver. Recipients whose caregiver develops multiple limitations are about twice as likely to report that help does not consistently meet their needs: more than one in two, compared with about one in four. More support around a couple does not mean that lost partner care has been replaced.

Hours You Cannot Choose: The Welfare Cost of Hours Constraints

Working paper, 2026
LISSUnderstanding Society

Many workers cannot choose their hours, yet standard welfare measures value their time at the wage. Using Dutch and British household panels, the paper shows that hours constraints are common, concentrated among higher earners and as costly for well-being as income poverty, and bounds the welfare loss at 6–32% of income for the median overemployed single.

Abstract

Welfare analysis usually assumes that the hours people work are the hours they chose. Revealed-preference methods, full-income measures and collective-household models all treat the budget set as the only restriction and value time at the wage. Yet hours come with jobs. Using household panels from the Netherlands (LISS) and the United Kingdom (Understanding Society), this paper shows that working more hours than desired is common, concentrated in the upper half of the income distribution, and costly: within person it lowers life satisfaction by half as much as falling into income poverty in the Netherlands and by twice as much in the United Kingdom. The constraint is real rather than a complaint. Hours barely move within a job but adjust sharply when workers change employer. A revealed-preference test on consumption and time-use data, with a built-in placebo, shows that the choices of the overemployed are rationalised by a shadow price of time above their wage and those of the underemployed below it. The paper brings quantity rationing into nonparametric revealed preference. This yields bounds on the virtual wage and on the money-metric loss without functional-form assumptions. For the median overemployed single, the loss is between 6% and 32% of income, a bracket that contains the well-being estimate. Valuing time at the wage overstates the welfare of constrained workers by exactly this loss. Ongoing work extends the analysis to couples, showing that stability-based identification of the intrahousehold sharing rule is invariant to rationed hours when time is private, and that households do not compensate the rationed spouse.

Income Risk Exposure and Consumption Smoothing in China

Working paper, 2026
CFPS 2012–2022

Low-income households face greater income risk without systematically weaker consumption smoothing. Identifying this exposure gap requires measuring income position before the shocks occur.

Abstract

Studies of consumption insurance often compare households by how strongly consumption responds to income shocks. This comparison can misstate distributional vulnerability when the size of the shocks also differs across households. Using six waves of the China Family Panel Studies from 2012 to 2022, this paper separately estimates households' exposure to permanent and transitory income shocks and the transmission of those shocks to consumption. Transitory income risk is consistently greater in the bottom income tercile. When income position is fixed using 2012–2014 resources and shocks are measured in later waves, transitory shock variance is 0.327 at the bottom and 0.234 at the top. Very low measured income realisations account for an important part of this difference. Consumption pass-through has no stable income ranking. A U-shaped profile appears when income position is measured outside each local shock window. It is absent when households are ranked before the shocks occur. The central distributional inequality in the data lies in exposure to income risk rather than systematically weaker consumption smoothing at the bottom. Comparing ex ante vulnerability therefore requires income rank to be fixed before the relevant shocks.

Presented at the Mannheim Applied Seminar (March 2026) and the ECARES Internal Seminar (February 2026).
