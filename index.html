About

I am a PhD candidate in economics at ECARES, Université libre de Bruxelles, supervised by Bram De Rock. I expect to complete my PhD in 2027 and am on the 2026–27 job market.

My research lies at the intersection of family economics, the economics of ageing and social insurance. I study how households insure themselves against income, employment and care shocks, and how informal insurance within the family interacts with public programmes. Using household survey data from Europe and China, I examine how families adjust consumption, labour supply and time use in response to these shocks, how care responsibilities are shared between spouses and across generations, and how policies such as short-time work schemes shape the degree of insurance households achieve.

Before joining ECARES, I studied at the Chinese University of Hong Kong and Georgetown University and worked at the Shenzhen Finance Institute, where I wrote reports on US macroeconomic developments and taught applied econometrics.

When Care Supply Becomes Care Demand: How Older Couples Adjust to Caregiver Decline

Working paper, October 2026Latest version (PDF)
SHARE

A healthy spouse is older people’s main insurance against long-term care needs, but the caregiving spouse is ageing too. When the caregiver’s own health declines sharply, outside help to the household rises, yet much of it goes to the caregiver rather than replacing the care the dependent partner has lost. Recipients become twice as likely to say that the help they get is not enough.

Abstract\n

A healthy spouse insures older people against long-term care needs, but the caregiving spouse is ageing too. Using SHARE, I follow couples in which one partner cares for the other as the caregiver develops new limitations in activities of daily living. One new limitation changes little. Two or more make the care relationship 20 percentage points more likely to end, lifting exit from about half to two-thirds. Children and home services then arrive together, not one in place of the other, and the former caregiver becomes a user of care: the share starting home personal care rises from one in a hundred to one in nine. New help for the original recipient mostly begins alongside help for the caregiver. Recipients who still need assistance are twice as likely to say that help does not consistently meet their needs: more than one in two, against one in four. Almost all of that is help received but insufficient, not help absent. Who gives the care matters, and so does where the couple lives. Exit rises 33 points when a husband is the caregiver, 10 when a wife is. In Central and Eastern Europe, where home personal care is scarce, declining caregivers rarely start receiving it, partner care does not end more often, and children step in instead. More support around a couple does not mean that lost partner care has been replaced.

Hours You Cannot Choose: The Welfare Cost of Hours Constraints

Working paper, November 2026
LISSUnderstanding Society

Four in ten Dutch workers would rather work fewer hours at their current wage, and their jobs do not let them. For a typical such worker, being able to choose her hours is worth about a seventh of her income, yet standard welfare measures, which value time at the wage, count this loss as zero.

Abstract

Welfare measures that include time assume that people work the hours they choose. Using household panels from the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, I show that many do not, and that the cost is large. Four in ten Dutch workers and more than a quarter of British employees would rather work fewer hours at their current wage. Their hours adjust mainly when they change employer, and in the Dutch data their consumption and time-use choices reveal a value of time above the wage; the reverse holds for workers who want more hours, a pattern mood would not produce. Extending nonparametric revealed preference to quantity rationing, I bound the loss without functional-form assumptions. For the median overemployed single it lies between a sixteenth and a third of income; life satisfaction, an independent measure, places it at about a seventh, inside this range. Standard measures set this loss to zero. Valuing time at the wage overstates the welfare of constrained workers, who are mostly neither poor nor working long hours, and hours-based time-poverty indices miss them. In couples, the error lies in the valuation of time, not in the inferred sharing of resources, and households do not compensate the rationed partner. Policies that let workers choose their hours yield gains these measures do not see.

Exposure or Pass-Through? Income Position and Consumption Risk in China

Working paper, November 2026
CFPS 2010–2022

Do low-income households bear more risk because they face bigger income shocks, or because they cope with them worse? In China, they face larger shocks, mostly short spells out of casual and migrant work, but show no sign of smoothing consumption less. Comparing groups by pass-through alone can therefore mislead, and the policy lesson is to stabilise income at its source.

Abstract

Do low-income households bear more consumption risk because they face larger income shocks or because they smooth those shocks less? A consumption model with persistent and short-lived income shocks shows that the answer requires measuring three things for each income group: the size of the shocks, their persistence, and how much of each kind reaches consumption. I measure all three using seven waves of the China Family Panel Studies (2010–2022), with households ranked by a prediction of permanent income made before the shocks. Two-year income changes of low-income households have nearly twice the variance of those of high-income households. The extra variation is short-lived and also appears in members’ own wage reports, so it is not mainly reporting noise. It consists largely of spells out of wage work: two years after leaving employment, 74 percent of bottom-tercile workers are employed again, against 47 percent at the top, and household income has recovered at the bottom but not at the top. Persistent shocks are of similar size across groups, their pass-through to consumption shows no income gradient, and consumption falls after job exits and illness at the top but not at the bottom. Public and private transfers absorb almost none of the shocks. Low-income households are disadvantaged in exposure to income risk, and the data show no evidence that they smooth it less. Weak restrictions on reporting error do not rank the two groups by the consumption risk that comes from income, and under several explicit measurement-error scenarios no difference between them is precisely estimated. The results point to policies that stabilise income at its source.
